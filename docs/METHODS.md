# Method

**Data and measure.** The camera data come from OpenStreetMap, where volunteers (mostly through the DeFlock project) map automated license plate readers. On October 3, 2026, I pulled every node tagged as a license plate reader in the 50 states and DC: 143,157 cameras. The list of agencies comes from the FBI's Crime Data Explorer directory and employee file (which gives population served), joined to the Bureau of Justice Statistics' agency crosswalk, which links each agency to a Census place or county. It holds 14,762 current local police departments, sheriff's offices and county police agencies, after removing 153 cities that the FBI directory lists as police departments but that pay the county sheriff for policing. I then credited each camera to the local agency its operator tag names, or, if it has none, to the agency whose area contains it: a city or town with its own police department, or else the county agency. Cameras tagged as belonging to someone else (a business, a homeowners' association, a state police force, a transportation agency, a university), or tagged as private, were left out, about 7,000 in all, as were 11 tagged as destroyed or disabled. A map point tagged as holding several cameras counts each one. Most cameras (84%) carry no operator tag at all. So the result is a count of mapped cameras in each agency's area, not an inventory of cameras the agency owns, and it says nothing about cameras an agency can search through shared networks. By this measure, 135,872 cameras are credited to 7,795 agencies, each camera to one agency only. That is 95% of all mapped cameras: untagged cameras that someone other than the police runs are counted for the agency whose area holds them. The median agency with at least one camera has 6 (the mean is 17); 62% have fewer than 10, and 1% have more than 181.

**Validation.** To test whether mapped counts are close to real ones, I collected publicly reported camera counts from agency websites, government documents and news articles quoting officials. Each of the 699 rows has a link and an exact quote from the source, checked against the page when collected; later rechecks of the 65 newest rows and a random sample of 40 found no mismatches. Crowdsourced maps and leaked data were not used. Flock's own transparency portals block automated access and were not used directly, though about 30 counts come from news reports that quote them. For the 360 agencies with a current program and a count of fixed cameras they operate, the mapped count is within a factor of two of the reported count for 75% of agencies, and within 20% for 40% (an agency reporting zero counts as within only if none are mapped either), and the correlation of the two (on a log scale) is 0.83. Where the agency's figure is a count of Flock cameras (305 of the 360), only cameras tagged as Flock are counted on the map side. Among the 342 agencies reporting at least one camera, the mapped count runs somewhat higher, by a median of 14%, and by a median of about 2 times for agencies serving 250,000 people or more, where cameras run by other parties are harder to separate out. A second check points the same way, more weakly: Minnesota's state register of fixed reader locations (correlation 0.52 over 83 agencies; the register counts locations, not cameras). Agreement is weaker for the largest agencies: for those serving 250,000 or more, the mapped count is within a factor of two only 41% of the time. Agencies with a published count are not a random sample: they are larger than most. Their reported counts have a median of 17 cameras (mean 37), and the same agencies have a median of 22 mapped cameras, against 6 for all agencies. So these checks show the mapped data are a reasonable guide to the typical agency, not that every agency's count is right.

**How the work was done.** I am Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. I wrote and ran this analysis in R, working in Claude Code at Charles Fain Lehman's direction. I also collected the reported counts, with several agents searching the web in parallel; the agents checked each quote against its source page. The work went through four rounds of adversarial review by separate Claude agents: an audit of the first version; four reviews of the code, the operator classifications, the validation data and the documentation; a line-by-line review of the code that recomputed the published numbers; and a second outside review of the department-level counts. Each found errors, which were fixed. The first audit's memo is in the repository's git history, and the later fixes are described in its commit messages. The code, the OpenStreetMap and FBI snapshots and the collected counts are in the project repository, and a full rerun reproduces every number above.
