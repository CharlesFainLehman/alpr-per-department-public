# Method

**Data and measure.** The camera data come from OpenStreetMap, where volunteers (mostly through the DeFlock project) map automated license plate readers. On October 3, 2026, I pulled every node tagged as a license plate reader in the 50 states and DC: 143,157 cameras. The list of agencies comes from the FBI's Crime Data Explorer directory and employee file (which gives population served), joined to the Bureau of Justice Statistics' agency crosswalk, which links each agency to a Census place or county. It holds 14,915 current local police departments, sheriff's offices and county police agencies. I then put each camera inside the boundaries of the agency whose area contains it: a city or town with its own police department, or else the county agency. Cameras whose operator tag names someone else (a business, a homeowners' association, a state police force, a transportation agency, a university) were left out, about 7,000 in all. Most cameras (84%) carry no operator tag at all. So the result is a count of mapped cameras in each agency's area, not an inventory of cameras the agency owns, and it says nothing about cameras an agency can search through shared networks. By this measure, 135,907 cameras fall in 7,941 agencies' areas; the median agency with at least one camera has 6.

**Validation.** To test whether mapped counts are close to real ones, I collected publicly reported camera counts from agency websites, government documents and news articles quoting officials. Each of the 699 rows has a link and an exact quote from the source, checked against the page when collected; later rechecks of the 65 newest rows and a random sample of 40 found no mismatches. Crowdsourced maps and leaked data were not used. Flock's own transparency portals block automated access and were not used directly, though about 30 counts come from news reports that quote them. For the 360 agencies with a current program and a count of fixed cameras they operate, the mapped count is within a factor of two of the reported count for 77% of agencies, and the correlation of the two (on a log scale) is 0.81. The mapped count runs somewhat higher, by a median of 14%, and by a median of about 1.9 times for agencies serving more than 250,000 people, where cameras run by other parties are harder to separate out. Two other checks point the same way: Flock portal counts scraped by The News & Observer in April 2024 (correlation 0.75 over 314 agencies), and, more weakly, Minnesota's state register of fixed reader locations (correlation 0.53 over 83 agencies; the register counts locations, not cameras). Agencies with a published count are not a random sample, so these checks show the mapped data are a reasonable guide, not that every agency's count is right.

**How the work was done.** I am Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic. I wrote and ran this analysis in R, working in Claude Code at Charles Fain Lehman's direction. I also collected the reported counts, with several agents searching the web in parallel; the agents checked each quote against its source page. The work went through two rounds of adversarial review: an audit of the first version, and later four independent reviews by separate Claude agents of the code, the operator classifications, the validation data and the documentation. Both found errors, which were fixed and are recorded in the repository. The code, the OpenStreetMap snapshot and the collected counts are in the project repository, and a full rerun from the snapshot reproduces every number above.
